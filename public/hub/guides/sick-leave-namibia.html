Sick leave in Namibia runs on a three year cycle, not a year

By the Katelago HR team

If you take one thing from this page, take this. Namibian sick leave is not an annual entitlement. It runs on a cycle of thirty six months.

This is the provision we see misquoted more often than any other, in contracts, in handbooks and in payroll systems that were configured for another country. It matters in both directions. Employers give away leave they never owed, and employees are refused leave they were entitled to.

What section 24 actually gives

During each sick leave cycle, an employee is entitled to not less than:

  • 30 working days of sick leave, if they ordinarily work five days a week
  • 36 working days, if they ordinarily work six days a week
  • a proportionate number of days, if they work fewer than five days a week

The sick leave cycle is defined in section 8 of the Labour Act as thirty six consecutive months of employment with the same employer, running from when employment started or from the end of the last cycle.

So what does that mean in practice?

Thirty working days over three years, not thirty a year. It works out at roughly ten days a year on average, but it is not allocated that way, and an employee may legitimately use a large part of the entitlement in one difficult year.

The first year is different

During an employee's first year of employment, the entitlement builds as they work. Section 24 gives one day of sick leave for every twenty six days worked.

This is the answer to the question every manager eventually asks, which is what happens when someone who started in February is off sick in March.

When you do not have to pay

Sick leave is paid at the employee's daily remuneration, on the normal pay day. Section 24 lists the situations where payment is not required:

  1. Where the employee is absent for more than two consecutive days and fails to produce a medical certificate from a medical practitioner, or other prescribed proof.
  2. To the extent that payment is made under the Employees Compensation Act, where the illness or injury is work related.
  3. To the extent that payment comes from a designated fund or organisation to which you contribute at least equally and which guarantees payment during sick leave.
  4. To the extent that payment is required under other legislation.

Read the first one carefully. The certificate requirement bites on absences of more than two consecutive days. A policy that demands a certificate for a single day is stricter than the Act, and while you may agree stricter terms, you cannot use the Act as your justification for refusing to pay.

Three rules that catch employers out

Unused sick leave is never paid out

Sick leave gives no additional remuneration on termination. It is not annual leave. An employee who leaves with twenty unused days walks away with nothing for them, and you should not be accruing a liability for it.

It lapses at the end of the cycle

Whatever is unused when the thirty six months close falls away. The new cycle starts fresh. It does not carry over.

It stands separately from other leave

Sick leave does not form part of annual leave, compassionate leave or maternity leave. You cannot set one off against another, and you cannot require an employee to use annual leave for a genuine illness.

And a fourth, which is really a termination point

Section 30 says you must not give notice of termination during any period of statutory leave, and you must not let notice run concurrently with it. That includes sick leave. If an employee is signed off, the notice waits.

We wrote about this in notice periods and the leave trap.

Where sick leave becomes an incapacity question

Long or repeated absence eventually stops being a leave question and becomes an incapacity question. That is a different process from misconduct, and a very different process from simply running out of patience.

Incapacity is not about fault. The fair route involves understanding the medical position, considering whether the work can be adapted, looking at alternatives, and only then considering termination. It is one of the areas where employers most often act too quickly and lose.

What to check this week

  1. Open your employment contract template and read the sick leave clause. If it says thirty days a year, it is wrong.
  2. Check how your payroll system is configured, particularly if it was set up outside Namibia.
  3. Check that you are tracking each employee's cycle start date, not the calendar year.
  4. Check that you are not accruing a payout liability for unused sick leave.

Sources
