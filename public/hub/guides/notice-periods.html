Notice periods, and the leave trap that catches good employers

By Damona Efraim, Managing Partner, Katelago HR Consultants

Notice looks like the simple part of a termination. It is where a surprising number of otherwise well handled exits go wrong, usually for one of three reasons: the period was wrong, the notice was not in writing, or it was given at the wrong moment.

The minimum periods

Section 30 of the Labour Act 11 of 2007 sets these minimums, based on how long the person has been employed.

Minimum notice by length of service
Length of serviceMinimum noticeWhen notice may be given
Four weeks or lessOne dayAny working day
More than four weeks, up to one yearOne weekOn or before the last working day of the week
More than one yearOne monthOn the first or the fifteenth of the month

That third column is not decoration. If you owe a month's notice and you hand it over on the ninth, the clock does not start until the fifteenth.

You may agree a longer period in the contract, but it has to be equal for both sides. You cannot bind an employee to two months while reserving one month for yourself.

Notice has to be in writing, with reasons

Section 30 requires notice to be in writing. Where the employer gives it, the notice must state the reasons for the termination and the date on which it is given.

That is a low bar and it is worth clearing carefully. A written notice with the reason on it, dated, and handed over with an acknowledgement of receipt, is the document you will be glad to have later.

There is one sensible exception. An employee who is illiterate may give notice orally.

The trap: notice and statutory leave

Section 30 says you must not give notice of termination during any period of leave the employee is entitled to under the Act, and you must not let notice run concurrently with such leave.

That covers annual leave, sick leave, compassionate leave and maternity leave. So you cannot hand someone their notice while they are signed off sick, and you cannot decide that their four weeks of annual leave will conveniently double as their notice month.

This is the mistake we are called about most often, and it is usually made by employers trying to be efficient rather than unfair.

Paying instead of giving notice

Section 31 allows you to pay the employee the remuneration they would have received during the notice period, instead of having them work it. That is lawful and often sensible.

It works in the other direction too. If the employee resigns and you would rather they left immediately, you may waive their notice, but then you must pay them for that period.

Payment in lieu deals with notice. It does not turn an unfair dismissal into a fair one. The reason and the process still have to stand up.

Summary dismissal

Nothing in section 30 takes away the right to terminate without notice for a cause recognised by law. Serious misconduct, established through a fair process, can justify dismissal without notice.

Be careful with the phrase "summary dismissal" though. It removes the notice period. It does not remove the requirement of a valid and fair reason and a fair procedure under section 33.

What counts as length of service

Section 29 is broader than people expect. The period of employment includes time actually worked, leave taken under the Act, any leave of absence granted, any period of suspension, the period between a dismissal and a reinstatement, and any lawful strike or lock out.

Get this right, because it drives both the notice period and severance.

Severance sits alongside notice

Under section 35, an employee who has completed twelve months of continuous service is entitled to severance pay if they are dismissed, if they die while employed, or if they resign or retire on reaching the age of sixty five.

The word dismissed is unqualified. Severance is not limited to retrenchment. The main exception is a fair dismissal on grounds of misconduct or poor work performance.

The calculation is at least one week's remuneration for each year of continuous service. Note remuneration, which is broader than basic wage, and note "at least", which is a floor rather than a ceiling.

Probation is not a defence

The word probation does not appear anywhere in the Labour Act. There is no statutory probation regime and no lower standard for dismissing a probationary employee. Section 33 applies from day one, and the onus of proving fairness sits with the employer from day one.

What does legitimately change during a probation period is the notice you owe, which is one day under four weeks of service and one week up to a year, and the fact that severance only arises after twelve months.

On the last day

Section 37 requires payment of all remuneration for work done, any untaken paid time off owing, and accrued annual leave for any completed cycle. The employee is also entitled to a certificate of employment.

Accrued annual leave is paid out on termination. Unused sick leave is not, and never is. We explain why in the guide on the sick leave cycle.

A short checklist

  1. Confirm the length of service, including suspensions and leave.
  2. Apply the correct minimum notice, and check the contract in case it says more.
  3. Check that the employee is not on statutory leave right now.
  4. Put the notice in writing, with the reason and the date.
  5. Give it on a permitted day for that category.
  6. Calculate severance if the person has twelve months or more of service.
  7. Prepare the final payment and the certificate of employment.
