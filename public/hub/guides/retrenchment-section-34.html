Retrenching one person triggers the same process as retrenching fifty

By the Katelago HR team

Let me start with the point that costs Namibian employers the most money.

Section 34 of the Labour Act contains no threshold. There is no minimum number of affected employees and no minimum size of employer. If you are letting someone go because the business is being re-organised or transferred, or because operations are being reduced or discontinued for economic or technological reasons, section 34 applies. Even if it is one person.

That includes informing the Labour Commissioner four weeks in advance. It includes negotiating in good faith. And until the process runs its course, you may not dismiss anybody.

Where the confusion comes from

South African law has a separate regime for large scale retrenchments that kicks in at fifty employees. Namibian employers and their advisers borrow that idea and assume something similar applies here. It does not. Our section 34 bites on the reason for the dismissal, not on the scale of it.

When section 34 applies

The trigger is the reason. Section 34 covers a reduction of the workforce arising from:

  • the re-organisation or transfer of the business, or
  • the discontinuance or reduction of the business for economic or technological reasons.

If the real reason is the employee's conduct or their performance, this is not a retrenchment and you should not dress it up as one. Retrenchment is about the post, not the person. Using it to move a difficult employee out quietly is one of the easiest things for an arbitrator to see through, and it converts a manageable problem into an unfair dismissal with severance attached.

What you have to do, and in what order

1. Inform, at least four weeks before

At least four weeks before the intended dismissals take place, you must inform:

  • the Labour Commissioner, and
  • any trade union recognised as the exclusive bargaining agent for those employees.

If there is no recognised bargaining agent, the same information goes to the workplace representatives elected under section 67 and to the employees themselves.

The information you must give is specific. The intended dismissals, the reasons for the reduction, the number and categories of employees affected, and the date on which the dismissals are intended to take place.

2. Disclose what they need to engage

You must disclose all relevant information necessary to allow the union or the representatives to engage effectively in the negotiations. This is not optional, and a consultation where the employer holds back the numbers is not a consultation.

There are limits. You do not have to disclose information that is legally privileged, that you are prohibited from disclosing by law or a court order, or that is confidential and would be likely to cause substantial harm to the business if it were disclosed. Those limits are real, but they are narrow, so use them carefully and be able to explain why.

3. Negotiate in good faith

The Act sets out exactly what you must negotiate about:

  1. alternatives to dismissal
  2. the criteria for selecting who is dismissed
  3. how to minimise the number of dismissals
  4. the conditions on which dismissals take place
  5. how to avert the adverse effects of the dismissals

Good faith is the operative phrase. Arriving at the meeting with the decision already made and the letters already typed is not negotiation, and it shows.

4. Select fairly

Employees must be selected according to criteria that are either agreed with the other side, or fair and objective. Last in first out is one option and it is defensible, but it is not the only lawful one. What matters is that the criteria are set in advance, applied consistently, and can be explained without embarrassment.

The moratorium: you cannot dismiss while the process is running

During the notification period and during any conciliation that follows, your duties to disclose and to negotiate continue, and you may not dismiss under section 34 unless the dispute has been settled or otherwise disposed of.

This is the provision employers breach most often, usually by treating the four weeks as a countdown to a decision already taken rather than as a period of genuine engagement.

If you cannot agree

If no agreement is reached, either party may refer the matter to the Labour Commissioner within one week after the notification period ends. The Commissioner must then appoint a conciliator, who convenes a meeting as soon as reasonably possible and may convene further meetings, up to a maximum of four weeks from the date of referral.

Plan your timeline on the basis that a contested retrenchment can take eight weeks or more from first notification. Building a business case that depends on people leaving at the end of the month is how employers end up cutting corners.

Closing down and quietly carrying on

Where an employer says it is closing or discontinuing operations, but in truth those operations continue under another name, in another form or at another location, without full disclosure, the employees or their bargaining agent may apply to the Labour Court. The relief available includes restoration of the operation, reinstatement, and lost and future earnings.

It is worth reading that twice before restructuring a group of companies.

What it costs when it goes wrong

Failing to comply with section 34 is an offence, punishable by a fine of up to N$10,000, or imprisonment of up to two years, or both. That is separate from anything an arbitrator awards.

And the employee keeps their other rights. Nothing in section 34 prevents them referring an unfair dismissal dispute, or a dispute about your failure to bargain in good faith, to the Labour Commissioner in the ordinary way.

Severance, notice and the rest of the money

Retrenchment is not one of the exclusions in section 35, so severance pay is due to every retrenched employee who has completed twelve months of continuous service. The minimum is one week's remuneration for each year of continuous service. Note remuneration rather than basic wage, and note that the section sets a floor rather than a ceiling.

Notice under section 30 applies on top of that, and you may not give notice during, or concurrently with, statutory leave. Final payments under section 37 include accrued annual leave and any outstanding time off, and the employee is entitled to a certificate of employment.

Maternity leave

An employee on maternity leave may not be dismissed on section 34 grounds during that leave, or at its expiry. If your selection pool includes someone on maternity leave, stop and get advice before you go further.

The human part, which is not in the Act

Retrenchment is the hardest thing we do in this profession. The law tells you the steps. It does not tell you how to sit across from someone who has been with the business for eleven years.

What I would say from experience is this. Tell people early and tell them the truth. Do not let them hear it in the corridor. Give real support with references, with the Social Security claim and with the next job. And be as generous as the business can honestly afford, because how you treat the people who leave is watched very closely by the people who stay.

Doing it lawfully and doing it decently are not in tension. They usually turn out to be the same thing.
