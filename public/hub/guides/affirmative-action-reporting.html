Affirmative Action reporting: what the Commission is actually looking for

By Damona Efraim, Managing Partner, Katelago HR Consultants

Two things about Affirmative Action reporting surprise employers. The first is how many of them are now caught by it. The second is that most reports that come back with queries fail on arithmetic, not on principle.

This guide covers both: who has to report, what goes into the report, and where reports actually go wrong.

Are you a relevant employer?

If you employ ten or more people, in any sector, you are a relevant employer under the Affirmative Action (Employment) Act 29 of 1998. That threshold has come down over the years, from fifty in 1999, to twenty five in 2006, to ten with effect from 15 September 2022.

A great many Namibian businesses crossed into scope in 2022 and have never filed anything. If that is you, the position is recoverable, but it is not something to leave for another year.

The three designated groups

  • racially disadvantaged persons
  • women, irrespective of race
  • persons with disabilities

Affirmative action means measures to ensure that people in these groups enjoy equal employment opportunities and are equitably represented at all levels of employment. An employment barrier is any rule, practice or condition, other than a legitimate job requirement, that affects designated groups more than others.

There is no national deadline, and that is the problem

This one catches good employers. There is no fixed date in the year when Affirmative Action reports are due.

  • Your first report, on Form EEC 1, is due within eighteen months of becoming a relevant employer.
  • Every further report, on Form EEC 2, is due twelve months after your previous submission.

So your due date is personal to you and it moves with your own filing history. If you need more time, apply with reasons at least two months before the due date. Do not apply the week it falls due.

Why it matters commercially

Without a valid Affirmative Action compliance certificate you cannot be given a State contract, guarantee, loan, licence, permit, grant or concession. For any business that tenders, the certificate is not a compliance nicety. It is the difference between bidding and not bidding.

The certificate runs from the date of issue until the Commission issues the next one, until a review panel finally disapproves your next report, or until you fail to submit a further report once twelve months have passed. Let the report slip and the certificate goes with it.

What you must actually have in place

The report is the visible part. Underneath it, the Act requires four things, and a report without them will not hold up.

  1. A senior employee appointed to manage implementation, and named in the report.
  2. A representative consultative committee, covering all occupational categories and both designated and non-designated groups, whose members are elected by the employees they represent.
  3. A statistical analysis of your workforce, showing where designated groups sit across occupational levels.
  4. A three year affirmative action plan, prepared in consultation with that committee.

What belongs in the plan

A plan that earns a clean certificate covers three years, identifies the actual barriers and imbalances in your workplace, sets yearly objectives, specifies concrete measures with strategies and methods, gives measurable targets and anticipated outcomes, names who is responsible for what, sets realistic time frames, states the actual numbers of designated group employees you are aiming for and when and how you will get there, demarcates the resources including a budget, and includes monitoring and dispute resolution procedures.

It is a working document, not a formality. The Commission can tell the difference in about four pages.

How the report is built

Form EEC 1 is the first report: the statistical report, the three year plan, a summary of measures and numerical goals, the names of non-Namibian employees and their understudies, and the records used to compile it.

Form EEC 2 is the annual further report. It contains everything in the first report, plus an evaluation of what you implemented in the preceding period, and a copy of the approved or amended plan.

The workforce grid

The statistical tables all use the same grid, which is worth knowing before you start collecting data. The columns run: job category, then racially disadvantaged men and women, racially advantaged men and women, persons with disabilities men and women, non-Namibians men and women, and totals.

The rows run down the occupational levels: executive directors, senior management, middle management, specialised or skilled or senior supervisory, skilled, semi-skilled, unskilled, total permanent, casual or temporary and seasonal, and total.

Every person sits in exactly one column set. A person with a disability is counted in the persons with disabilities column, not also in the racially disadvantaged column. Non-Namibians appear only in their own column. Use zero where a cell is nil rather than leaving it blank.

Five things that get a report sent back

1. The numbers do not agree with each other

This is the biggest one by a distance. One workforce number has to run through the entire report. If the workforce profile says eighteen, the training list says nineteen and the salary table says twenty two, the report will come back. Movement must balance too: opening headcount, plus recruitment, less terminations, equals closing headcount.

2. Table 8 is filled in as a list of HR problems

Table 8 asks in which categories of employment practice you will implement affirmative action measures. It is not a list of your general HR shortcomings. Mark yes only where a genuine barrier to designated groups exists, in entry, promotion or continued employment. The final column should carry the measure or good practice you will apply, not a description of the fault. And every yes must have a matching measure in Annexure D, because the review officer checks that mapping.

3. The numerical goals are empty, or do not reconcile

Blank goal tables read as no plan at all. The arithmetic has to work: your current workforce, plus the additions in year one, year two and year three, must equal the envisaged profile at the end of the plan.

Keep the goals realistic. The Act confirms that an employer is not required to create posts or hire an arbitrary number of people. For a smaller business, modest representation goals funded by natural turnover are far more defensible than invented headcount growth.

4. The committee is management only

A consultative committee made up entirely of managers is not compliant and will be flagged. Members should be elected by the employees they represent, the committee should cover all occupational categories, and signed minutes with an attendance register have to be attached. State how often it meets, quarterly being the usual answer.

5. The reporting period is stale

The period must be a genuine twelve months immediately preceding the report. Carrying last year's period forward is an obvious tell, and it invites the review officer to look harder at everything else.

How the Commission decides

A review officer reads the report and decides whether you have adopted and are implementing a plan, and whether you have complied with the Act. In doing so they weigh your good faith effort, prevailing economic conditions, and whether suitably qualified designated group candidates were actually available.

There are three outcomes. The Commission approves the report and issues a compliance certificate. Or it approves conditionally, where you undertake in writing to remedy the shortcomings. Or it disapproves and refers the report to a review panel.

There is no A, B or C grade, whatever you may have been told. A good outcome means an unconditional certificate on first review, with no queries.

If you have had a shortcomings letter

Close every point explicitly, and say where in the new report you have done it. Never resubmit with a previously flagged error still in place. Repeating a fault the Commission already identified, particularly a reconciliation error between the workforce and salary tables, is the surest way to lose a clean certificate.

What a lawful plan does not do

This matters, because it is where a lot of quiet anxiety sits among Namibian managers, and it is rarely said plainly.

A lawful affirmative action plan does not require you to dismiss employees who are not in a designated group, and it does not create an absolute barrier to their advancement. Anyone advanced under the plan must be suitably qualified. The Act is about removing barriers and creating equal opportunity, not about causing a new unfairness.

The strongest plans I have worked on are the ones where management stopped treating the report as a submission and started treating it as a description of how they intend to build the business. Those are also, reliably, the ones that come back approved.

A practical order of work

  1. Confirm your report type and your own twelve month reporting period.
  2. Establish one authoritative workforce number and grade every person to their real occupational level.
  3. Constitute or refresh the consultative committee, by election, and minute the meetings.
  4. Do the statistical analysis and identify the genuine barriers.
  5. Build the plan, with measures mapped to those barriers and numerical goals that reconcile.
  6. Write an honest evaluation of the preceding year, including what did not happen and why.
  7. Reconcile every table against every other table before anything is printed.
