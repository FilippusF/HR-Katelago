Employing non-Namibians in oil and gas, and the understudy rule everyone forgets

By the Katelago HR team

Every serious conversation about Namibian oil and gas eventually reaches the same point. The skills are not all here yet, so specialists have to be brought in, and everyone agrees that should be temporary. The labour ministry put it plainly in August 2026: importing skills must be an interim measure, not a permanent substitute for local capacity.

The question is what that actually requires of an employer. Here it is.

The permit side

Employing a non-Namibian requires an employment permit under the Immigration Control Act 7 of 1993. Before the Immigration Selection Board issues one, it must be satisfied of three things.

  1. That the applicant has the qualifications, education, training or experience likely to make them efficient in the job.
  2. That the employment is not one in which enough people are already engaged in Namibia to meet the country's requirements. This is the labour market test, and it is the one that fails applications.
  3. That issuing the permit would not conflict with the Act or any other law.

The Board may set the period and the conditions of the permit. In practice, employers are expected to show why the foreign skill is needed, what efforts were made to recruit locally, and how the skill will be transferred to Namibians. Build that evidence pack before you apply rather than after the query letter arrives.

New from 1 September 2026

A new employment permit to conduct business takes effect on 1 September 2026, valid for up to five years rather than the usual two.

It is not open to everyone. It is restricted to investors whose investor status is confirmed by the Namibia Investment Promotion and Development Board, which conducts an economic evaluation before the application goes to the Chief of Immigration and the Selection Board. Applications are submitted manually through the Board. Employers who are not investors continue on the existing employment permit route.

If your business is an investor in the sector and you are carrying two year permit renewal cycles for senior people, this is worth looking at now.

A permit is not the end of the obligation

Getting the permit is the part employers plan for. The part they forget sits in a different Act entirely, and it is the one that shows up later in an Affirmative Action review.

The understudy rule

Section 19(3) of the Affirmative Action (Employment) Act 29 of 1998 says that a relevant employer must train a Namibian citizen as the understudy of every non-Namibian citizen it employs.

Read that again. Every one. Not a general commitment to skills transfer, and not a training budget. A named Namibian, being trained, for each non-Namibian on your payroll.

The Minister may grant an exemption where the trade or profession is too specialised, or where training is impractical. That exemption has to be applied for. It does not apply simply because the role is difficult.

This obligation is enforced through the affirmative action reporting cycle. Your report names your non-Namibian employees and their understudies, and describes the training programme. A report that shows expatriates with no understudies, and no exemption, is an obvious finding for a review officer.

Why this bites hardest in oil and gas

Without a valid Affirmative Action compliance certificate, an employer cannot be given a State contract, guarantee, loan, licence, permit, grant or concession. In a sector where the State is a partner in every block through NAMCOR, and where licences and permits are the currency of doing business, that is not a small consequence.

What a real understudy programme looks like

A named person on a form is not a programme. What holds up under scrutiny, and what actually builds capability, is this.

  1. A defined target role, so that everyone knows what the understudy is training towards.
  2. A skills gap analysis against that role, in writing.
  3. A structured development plan with milestones, including formal training, on the job exposure and progressive responsibility.
  4. Named accountability, meaning the expatriate holder has skills transfer written into their own performance agreement, not just goodwill.
  5. Regular documented reviews, with evidence kept.
  6. A realistic handover date, and a succession plan that says what happens on it.

The fifth point is where most programmes quietly fail. If transferring the skill is not part of how the expatriate is measured and paid, the transfer competes with the day job and loses.

The vacancy notification duty

There is a further obligation worth knowing about, although its practical status needs checking for your own business.

Part 3 of the Employment Services Act 8 of 2011 requires designated employers to notify the national employment bureau of every vacancy or new position, temporary or permanent, no later than the date the advertisement is placed. A designated employer may not fill a position without considering in good faith any suitably qualified job seeker referred to it, and must report back whether that person was employed. Designated employers must also submit an annual establishment profile showing, among other things, the number of Namibian and non-Namibian employees by occupation.

Non-compliance carries a fine of up to N$20,000 or imprisonment of up to two years, or both.

The honest caveat is that whether these duties bite on you depends on whether you have been designated by the Minister, and the administration of this Act has been uneven. Confirm your own position with the labour ministry rather than assuming either way. What we would not advise is ignoring it because it is inconvenient to check.

What the licence itself requires

If you are working for or with a licence holder, remember that section 14 of the Petroleum Act already makes it a condition of every licence that the holder gives preference to suitably qualified Namibian citizens in employment, and carries out training programmes to develop them.

Operators pass those conditions down through their contracts. So even where a subcontractor is not itself a licence holder, the workforce commitments tend to arrive contractually. Read the labour and training clauses of any oil and gas contract before you sign it, not after.

The bigger point

There is a version of this conversation that treats the understudy rule as a compliance nuisance, and there is a version that treats it as the whole reason the sector matters to Namibia.

National planning targets a rise from roughly 2,800 jobs in the sector in 2024 to around 22,800 by 2030. Whether that happens does not depend on how many barrels are in the ground. It depends on whether there are Namibians qualified and experienced enough to be appointed when the work arrives, and the only way that happens is if employers train them now, deliberately, while there is still time.

An understudy programme done properly is not paperwork. It is one Namibian getting a career they would not otherwise have had. Multiply that by every expatriate on every project and you have the sector this country has been promised.
